# Synodic Board – ideas for games

*[Wersja polska](ideas_pl.md)*

Themes and elements that follow from the board's physics and can inspire games on it. These are not rules – just material to use.

## Game rhythm

- A typical interesting game is more like 30–50 rounds. The rounds themselves should be simple and short.
- Interesting places in the asteroid belt can appear at random and stay on the board for some time – for example on the order of 10 rounds.

## Mission success versus the return

When a ship finishes its work in the asteroid belt, the Earth is somewhere else entirely. The tension between exploiting the belt efficiently and getting back in a good transfer window can be one of the main dramas of a game. A player who misses the window chooses: wait (at the cost of time) or fly a more expensive route (at the cost of fuel).

## Ceres as an expedition target

Ceres can be tracked separately, with its own drift (2 hexes backward every 3 rounds), and visiting it can require a lander carried by the ship. An expedition to Ceres then has to be planned: the target moves, and a lander is needed on arrival.

A question for a particular game to settle: why not keep a lander at Ceres permanently? Where a permanent settlement has grown up, landers would probably be local; a ship's own lander fits the early days of belt exploration.

## A station at Mars

Returning from the asteroid belt to Mars orbit costs 2–2.3 times less fuel than returning to the Earth, but the window to Mars comes three times less often (every 18 rounds instead of every 6) and the flight takes longer. A station at Mars does not pay off as a stopover on the way to the Earth (belt → Mars → Earth costs as much as a direct flight), but it does pay off as a destination or a fuel station – especially since lifting cargo to orbit from the surface of Mars is much cheaper than from the Earth.

## Scenario dials

Several parameters of the model work well for configuring different scenarios:

- water extraction rate and water content of asteroids (higher in the outer part of the belt),
- availability of mining stations, orbital stations and landers,
- tank capacity and the effect of cargo on range,
- tracking or ignoring drift in the belt.

## Regular lines versus transfer windows

Names and marketing can promise regular services – and transfer windows don't allow them. The clash between passengers' expectations and the physics of flight can be a theme of play. A historical counterpart: the Space Shuttle was sold as a regular, cheap "space truck" – dozens of launches a year were planned, and it ended up with 135 flights in 30 years.
